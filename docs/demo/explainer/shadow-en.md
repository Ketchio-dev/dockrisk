# Shadow run — the talk, spoken in his own voice, to speak along with

This is not a film to show anyone. It is a model run: the screens in the order they will be
clicked, and the words over them in his own cloned voice, at the pace he should speak. He plays
it, speaks along, and stops needing the paper.

Written against what the research says actually works for a non-native speaker under stress:

- **Short sentences. One verb each.** No sentence with three verbs (YC partner's rule).
- **No word he would not use in conversation.** The clever line is worth less than the line he
  can say without thinking.
- **130–150 words per minute.** TED averages 163 and those are native speakers on their best day.
- **The screen holds the numbers.** He points and reads what is displayed. A number on screen
  cannot be forgotten.
- **Two sentences are memorised** — the first and the last. Everything else is a place to point at.

Beat names match `scripts/record_shadow.sh`. Only `>` lines are spoken.

---

### 01 · Open

> Hi. I'm Junsu.
> A truck waiting at a dock loses its next load before it earns a detention charge.
> DockRisk catches that moment. It tells the dispatcher which truck to move.
> That's the whole idea. Let me show you where it comes from.

### 02 · The finding

> This is the carrier's own file. Fifty-six days. Every stop replayed through our rules.
> Thirty-two to forty-three thousand dollars a month of detention exposure.
> I want to be careful here. This is exposure. It is not money you can collect.
> The file has no billing records, so we do not call it unbilled revenue.

### 03 · Where the money sits

> Look at this chart. Most waits end inside an hour.
> The money is in the thin tail, on the right of the free-time line.
> That tail is what manual logging misses. Nobody writes down a two-hour wait at the time.

### 04 · The board

> This is the dispatcher's desk.
> Eight trucks, ranked by which one becomes a problem first.
> Only the top row is open. If everything is open, nothing is urgent.
> This driver is at a London dock. He has been waiting since nine twenty-five.

### 05 · The day bar

> This bar is one driver's day on a time axis.
> Black is driving. Grey is on duty. The hatched part is his two free hours.
> After the hatch, amber. Amber is money.
> And this red tick is when he must stop driving by law.
> Now look at the order. The red tick comes first.
> That is the problem. He loses the next load before the charge even starts.

### 06 · The verdict

> The product does not say "at risk". It says what to do.
> Reassign this load now. The load number is right there.
> And underneath, it says why.
> Even if we release him this second, he is still short.

### 07 · The driver

> This is what the driver sees on his phone.
> Hours at the top. His duty log underneath.
> He taps once to confirm he arrived.
> That tap is evidence. It sets when the billing clock starts, and it records who said so.
> This is a companion app. It is not a certified ELD. We do not claim that.

### 08 · The rescue

> The next load is in trouble, so we look for a relief driver.
> Candidates come back with reasons, not scores.
> Distance. Arrival time. Trailer type. Whether his hours cover the whole trip.
> Drivers who cannot make it are blocked, and it says why.

### 09 · The dock line

> Now read this line under each driver. This is the part I am most proud of.
> The plan gives every dock forty-five minutes. That is the industry default.
> But this lane's real docks take longer. We know, because it is in the file.
> Costed that way, this driver is eleven minutes past a legal stop.
> Four of seven candidates flip.
> The decision still uses the fixed rule. The history sits beside it, with its sample size.

### 10 · The offer

> One offer goes out. The driver accepts on his phone.
> The load is saved. The original stop keeps all of its evidence.

### 11 · The money

> He is released, and the charge writes itself.
> Fifteen billable minutes. Eighteen dollars and seventy-five cents.
> That is a small number. That is the point.
> Nobody chases eighteen dollars by hand. That is why it is never billed.

### 12 · The packet

> Here is what sits behind that amount.
> The calculation. The timeline. And who reported each event.
> We compute the amount automatically. We never bill automatically.
> A person presses the button.

### 13 · The AI

> There are two places we use a model, and this is one.
> It reads a rate confirmation and pulls out the terms.
> It shows the sentence it used, so you check the contract, not the model.
> Rules do the law and the money. The model only reads text and writes text.

### 14 · The limits

> Here is what we did not build, on purpose.
> Axle weight. The truck sheet has one column. There is nothing to compute from.
> We do not report revenue. The file has no rates.
> And stops over six hours never bill themselves.
> Forty-nine of those stops carry two thirds of all the hours. We do not pretend they are all real.

### 15 · Close

> So that is DockRisk.
> One dock delay becomes three things. A charge with its evidence. A forward check on the next load. And a driver who can still take it.
> Watch the clock that runs out first.
> Thank you.
